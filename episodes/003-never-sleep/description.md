# What if you never slept again?

## Description

Randy Gardner was seventeen when he decided to stay awake for eleven days.
By day two his eyes stopped focusing. By day four he was seeing people who
weren't there. By day eleven he forgot what he was doing in the middle of
a simple counting task.

And then he slept for fourteen hours and was completely fine.

That's the problem.

This is what actually happens when you don't sleep — the chemical that builds
up in your brain, the cleaning that only happens at night, the small pieces of
your brain that fall asleep while you're standing up, and the people who
couldn't stop.

## Sources

- Randy Gardner, 1964 — 264 hours (11 days), age 17, supervised by sleep
  researchers. Slept ~14 hours afterwards, fully recovered. (NPR, 2017)
- Day-by-day symptoms: eye focus (day 2), mood (day 3), hallucinations (day 4),
  slurred speech and memory failures (days 6–8), paranoia (day 10), stopped
  counting at 65 on the final day. (Stanford sleep lab records)
- Robert McDonald, 1986 — 453 hours 40 minutes. Guinness stopped monitoring the
  record in 1997. (Guinness World Records)
- Adenosine accumulates during wakefulness as the homeostatic sleep-pressure
  signal; caffeine is an adenosine receptor antagonist that blocks the signal
  without removing the pressure. (Reichert et al., *Journal of Sleep Research*, 2022)
- Glymphatic clearance: interstitial space increases ~60% during sleep,
  increasing cerebrospinal fluid exchange and β-amyloid clearance.
  (Xie et al., *Science*, 2013)
- Fatal familial insomnia: inherited prion disease, thalamic neuronal loss,
  mean course 18 months, no cure. (NCBI Bookshelf NBK482208)
- Total sleep deprivation in rats: death after ~2–3 weeks, weight loss despite
  increased food intake, body temperature decline, skin lesions.
  (Rechtschaffen & Bergmann, 1996)
- No documented human death from sleep deprivation alone.
